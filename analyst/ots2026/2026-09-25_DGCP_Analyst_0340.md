### System Context

Livelihoods remain one of the most important yet least visible structures supporting modern civilization.

Individuals depend on livelihoods.

Households depend on livelihoods.

Communities depend on livelihoods.

Civilization depends on systems through which people can secure resources for daily life so that basic needs and participation can be supported across time.

Many observers focus on ways to earn a living, jobs, income, occupations, businesses, production, or other visible expressions associated with livelihoods.

The discussion frequently centers on how people secure resources.

The structural capability through which people can maintain means of securing resources for daily life receives less attention.

While reviewing developments across households, employment, production, services, communities, markets, social systems, and other areas involving daily economic participation, I found myself paying less attention to individual ways of earning a living and more attention to the livelihood structures through which sustained means of support can be maintained.

Ways to earn a living remained visible.

Sustained means of support became visible.

The observation was not about ways to earn a living.

The observation was about livelihoods.

### Structural Analysis

Most people see ways to earn a living.

Systems thinkers see livelihood architecture.

Livelihoods appear as the structures through which people can secure resources for daily life so that basic needs and participation can be supported across time.

Their significance extends beyond employment or income alone.

Behind means of securing resources for daily life exist livelihoods.

Behind sustained means of support exist livelihoods.

Behind potential support for basic needs can exist livelihoods.

Behind continued participation can exist livelihoods.

Behind connections between daily resources and social continuity can exist livelihoods.

Behind connections between daily resources and economic continuity can exist livelihoods.

The relationship is structural.

Livelihoods provide means through which people can secure resources for daily life.

Sustained access to resources can support basic needs and participation.

Continued participation can contribute to social and economic continuity.

Social and economic continuity therefore depend partly on whether people can maintain means of securing resources for daily life.

The dependency extends across multiple systems simultaneously.

Individuals depend on livelihoods.

Households depend on livelihoods.

Communities depend on livelihoods.

Economic systems interact with livelihoods.

Social systems depend partly on continued participation that livelihoods can support.

Viewed independently, these systems involve different people, resources, needs, activities, and forms of participation.

Viewed together, they reveal livelihoods as support infrastructure connecting means of securing resources for daily life, basic needs, potential continued participation, and social and economic continuity.

Ways to earn a living remained visible.

Livelihood architecture became visible.

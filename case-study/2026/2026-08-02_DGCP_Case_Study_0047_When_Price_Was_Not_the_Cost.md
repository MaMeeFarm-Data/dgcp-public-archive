# DGCP™ Case Study #0047

## When Price Was Not the Cost

### How Price and Total Cost Can Represent Different Parts of the Same System

> *"Price is one observable measure within a transaction. Total cost can extend beyond the initial price as operational, time, risk, quality, impact, and end-of-life factors emerge across the lifecycle."*

## Purpose

This case study examines how price and total cost can represent different parts of the same system.

It observes the relationships among acquisition cost, operating cost, time cost, risk cost, quality and impact cost, end-of-life cost, lifecycle effects, trade-offs, and system outcomes.

---

## Core Question

What is the difference between price and the total cost experienced by a system?

---

## Core Observation

Price is an observable amount associated with a transaction.

It may represent the amount paid to acquire a product, service, asset, resource, or capability at a particular point in time.

The total cost experienced by a system can extend beyond that initial transaction.

Operation and maintenance may require additional resources. Delays, downtime, rework, or lost opportunities may introduce time-related costs. Uncertainty and disruption can create additional exposure. Quality differences may affect maintenance, replacement, reliability, or operational performance.

End-of-life handling, disposal, replacement, transition, or decommissioning may also create costs that were not visible at the time of acquisition.

The significance of each factor varies across systems and contexts.

Price and total cost therefore describe different views of economic and operational experience within a system. One is visible at the transaction; the other may develop across the lifecycle.

---

## Price vs Total Cost

1. **Price**  
   The amount paid in a transaction.

2. **Total Cost**  
   The combined costs and impacts experienced over time within the system.

Price can form one component of total cost, while additional factors may emerge during operation, use, maintenance, disruption, adaptation, replacement, or end-of-life activities.

---

## Components That May Contribute to Total Cost

1. **Acquisition Cost**  
   The initial price or purchase cost associated with obtaining an asset, product, service, or capability.

2. **Operation Cost**  
   Resources required to operate, support, and maintain the system over time.

3. **Time Cost**  
   Costs or impacts associated with delays, downtime, rework, waiting, or lost opportunities.

4. **Risk Cost**  
   Potential costs associated with uncertainty, disruption, failure, or other adverse events.

5. **Quality and Impact Cost**  
   Additional costs or consequences associated with quality differences, performance limitations, defects, or operational impacts.

6. **Disposal and End-of-Life Cost**  
   Costs associated with replacement, disposal, decommissioning, transition, recycling, or other end-of-life activities.

The combination and significance of these factors can vary across systems, industries, operating environments, and lifecycle stages.

---

## Observable Signals

- Lower-priced options later requiring additional operating or maintenance expenditure.
- Delays, downtime, or rework creating additional time-related costs.
- Disruptions generating costs beyond the original transaction.
- Quality issues contributing to maintenance, replacement, or other additional expenses.
- Replacement, transition, disposal, or end-of-life costs becoming visible after acquisition.

---

## Structural Perspective

A total-cost perspective can include several interconnected components:

- Transaction
- Operation
- Time
- Risk
- Quality and impact
- End-of-life

The transaction establishes an initial observable price.

Operation can introduce recurring resource requirements.

Time can influence the effects of delays, downtime, and process duration.

Risk can introduce potential costs that may or may not materialize.

Quality and performance can influence maintenance, reliability, replacement, and operational outcomes.

End-of-life activities can introduce additional requirements after the primary period of use.

These components illustrate why the initial transaction price may represent only one part of the wider cost structure experienced by a system.

- Observe trade-offs among cost, quality, time, and risk.

- Observe how different cost components influence system outcomes over time.

---

## Key Insight

Price is visible at the transaction.

Total cost may become visible progressively through operation, time, risk, quality, maintenance, disruption, replacement, and end-of-life activities.

The distinction between price and total cost provides two different observational views of the same system: the immediate transaction and the broader lifecycle experience.

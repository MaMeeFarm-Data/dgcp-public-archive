### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2621: Cascading Effects

Cascading effects occur when a change in one part of a system produces successive effects across connected components, processes, networks, or systems.

Each affected component can become a pathway through which additional effects emerge elsewhere.

Dependencies, coupling, flows, interfaces, shared resources, and operational sequences connect successive stages of a cascade.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2622: Cascade Initiation

Cascade initiation occurs when an initial change begins a sequence of connected system effects.

The initiating condition may involve capacity, resources, infrastructure, flows, information, institutions, environmental conditions, operational activity, or functional loss.

Identifying the initiating condition distinguishes the beginning of a cascade from subsequent system responses.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2623: Cascade Pathways

Cascade pathways are the relationships through which successive effects move across interconnected systems.

Dependencies, networks, infrastructure links, supply chains, interfaces, shared services, information systems, and operational sequences can provide pathways.

A single initiating condition can enter multiple pathways and produce different sequences of effects.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2624: Cascade Stages

Cascade stages are successive system states or operational effects connected to an initiating change.

An initial disruption can affect an immediate dependency, which can alter a connected process, which can subsequently change conditions elsewhere.

Stages make the sequence of connected effects observable across time and system structure.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2625: First Order Effects

First order effects are changes directly connected to an initiating condition.

Loss of a required input can immediately reduce the operation of a dependent process while a constrained node can directly alter connected flows.

These effects represent the first observable stage beyond the initiating change.

First order effects remain observable.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2626: Second Order Effects

Second order effects occur when first order changes alter conditions within additional connected components or systems.

Reduced production can change transport activity, inventory movement, resource use, or downstream processing.

Second order effects extend a cascade beyond the initial dependency relationship.

Second order effects remain observable.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2627: Higher Order Effects

Higher order effects emerge through successive relationships beyond the first and second stages of a cascade.

Effects can move through multiple dependencies, networks, organizations, locations, and operational domains before becoming observable.

Increasing cascade depth can separate later effects from the original initiating condition.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2628: Cascade Depth

Cascade depth describes the number of successive stages through which connected effects extend.

Shallow cascades involve a limited number of dependent relationships while deeper cascades move through multiple system layers.

Dependency structure and operational connectivity influence the observable depth of a cascade.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2629: Cascade Breadth

Cascade breadth describes the number or range of components, networks, locations, activities, or systems affected across a cascade.

A narrow cascade may remain within one operational chain while a broader cascade can extend across multiple parallel or cross-system relationships.

Shared dependencies and network branching can increase the distribution of effects.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2630: Cascade Velocity

Cascade velocity describes the rate at which successive effects become observable across connected system stages.

Tight coupling, limited buffers, rapid information transfer, synchronized processes, or direct dependencies can shorten intervals between effects.

Inventories, reserves, processing times, institutional procedures, or loose coupling can extend those intervals.

Cascade velocity remains observable.

I log cascade velocity.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2631: Cascade Amplification

Cascade amplification occurs when successive effects increase in scale, intensity, or operational significance as they move through connected systems.

High utilization, concentrated dependencies, limited alternatives, feedback, shared constraints, or tight coupling can contribute to amplification.

The scale of later effects can therefore differ from the initiating change.

Cascade amplification remains observable.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2632: Cascade Dampening

Cascade dampening occurs when successive effects become smaller as they move through connected system relationships.

Buffers, spare capacity, inventories, redundancy, substitution, alternative pathways, distributed resources, or operational flexibility can reduce transmitted effects.

Dampening changes the intensity of a cascade without necessarily ending it.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2633: Cascade Branching

Cascade branching occurs when effects from one stage move into multiple connected pathways.

A constrained resource, failed node, disrupted service, or changed flow can affect several dependent activities simultaneously.

Branching expands the structural reach of a cascade across networks and systems.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2634: Cascade Convergence

Cascade convergence occurs when effects originating from multiple pathways combine at a shared component, node, process, resource, or system.

Separate disruptions can place simultaneous demands on the same transport route, infrastructure service, institution, resource, or operational capacity.

Convergence connects multiple effect pathways within a common system location or function.

Temporal cascades occur when successive effects become observable across different periods.

Inventories, reserves, transport times, production cycles, contracts, maintenance schedules, reporting intervals, and institutional processes can separate cascade stages in time.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2638: Cascade Interruption

Cascade interruption occurs when a pathway connecting successive effects is broken, isolated, substituted, or made operationally irrelevant.

Alternative resources, independent capacity, rerouting, segmentation, redundancy, substitution, or changed operating procedures can interrupt transmission.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2639: Cascade Boundaries

Cascade boundaries identify points where successive effects stop, weaken substantially, become isolated, or cease to remain operationally relevant.

Boundaries can arise from system separation, independent capacity, alternative pathways, redundancy, buffers, limited coupling, or disconnected dependencies.

Observed boundaries define the structural reach of a cascade within interconnected systems.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2640: Cascading Effects Framework

Cascading effects connect initiation, pathways, stages, dependencies, networks, interfaces, shared resources, and operational sequences across interconnected systems.

First order, second order, and higher order effects describe successive stages while depth, breadth, and velocity describe the structural and temporal extent of a cascade.

Amplification, dampening, branching, convergence, spatial movement, temporal progression, interruption, and boundaries describe how cascading effects develop and change across system structures.

Together these relationships reveal how an initial change can generate successive and distributed effects across multiple components, locations, networks, and system domains.

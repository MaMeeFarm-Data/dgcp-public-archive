### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2601: Failure Propagation

Failure propagation describes how loss or degradation of function in one component can affect other connected components, processes, networks, or systems.

Failures can originate within infrastructure, equipment, software, resources, services, organizations, institutions, or operational processes.

Dependencies, coupling, interfaces, and shared resources provide pathways through which functional loss can extend beyond its point of origin.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2602: Failure Origin

Failure origin identifies the component, process, asset, service, interface, or condition where functional loss or degradation begins.

Origins can occur through physical damage, technical malfunction, resource loss, connectivity interruption, operational error, software failure, or unavailable supporting services.

Identifying the origin separates the initial failure from subsequent effects across connected systems.

Failure pathways are the connections through which functional loss becomes relevant to other parts of a system.

Dependencies, interfaces, infrastructure links, service relationships, supply chains, digital networks, shared resources, and operational sequences can provide propagation pathways.

A single failure can connect with multiple pathways simultaneously.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2604: Direct Failure Propagation

Direct failure propagation occurs when loss of function immediately affects a connected component, process, service, or system.

Failure of a required input, interface, connection, or supporting service can prevent the next dependent function from operating as required.

Direct dependencies create identifiable relationships between initial and subsequent failures.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2618: Failure Containment

Failure containment occurs when propagated functional loss is limited within a defined portion of a system.

Operational controls, rerouting, substitution, reserve capacity, shutdown procedures, network segmentation, or independent resources can reduce further propagation.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2619: Propagation Boundaries

Propagation boundaries identify points where failure effects stop, weaken, become isolated, or cease to affect required functions elsewhere.

Independent capacity, alternative resources, redundant infrastructure, modular design, limited coupling, segmentation, or disconnected dependencies can create such boundaries.

Observed boundaries distinguish affected system structures from functions that remain operational.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2620: Failure Propagation Framework

Failure propagation connects origins, pathways, dependencies, interfaces, nodes, links, networks, shared resources, and supporting services across interconnected systems.

Failure can propagate directly, indirectly, upstream, downstream, laterally, and across system boundaries while network structure influences the distribution of functional loss.

Amplification can extend failure effects while isolation, containment, redundancy, and propagation boundaries can limit their reach.

Together these relationships reveal how loss of function can move beyond its point of origin and alter operations across connected components, networks, and system layers.

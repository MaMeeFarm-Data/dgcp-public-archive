### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2581: Shock Propagation

Shock propagation describes how a discrete or rapid change originating within one part of a system becomes transmitted through connected components, networks, processes, or systems.

Shocks can originate from physical disruption, resource change, capacity loss, infrastructure interruption, information events, financial conditions, institutional action, environmental events, or operational failure.

Dependencies, coupling, flows, interfaces, and networks provide pathways through which shock effects can extend beyond their point of origin.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2582: Shock Origin

Shock origin identifies the component, event, location, process, or external condition from which a rapid system change begins.

Origins may occur within infrastructure, resources, markets, institutions, environments, technologies, organizations, or operational processes.

Identifying the origin distinguishes the initiating change from subsequent transmitted effects.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2583: Shock Pathways

Shock pathways are the connections through which effects move from an originating disturbance toward other parts of a system.

Dependencies, supply chains, infrastructure links, financial relationships, information networks, shared resources, and operational sequences can provide propagation pathways.

Multiple pathways can transmit effects from the same originating shock.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2584: Direct Shock Propagation

Direct shock propagation occurs when a disturbance affects an immediately connected component, process, node, or system.

Loss of an input, connection, service, or operating function can alter conditions at the next dependent stage.

Direct dependencies create identifiable pathways between an originating disturbance and its immediate effects.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2585: Indirect Shock Propagation

Indirect shock propagation occurs when effects move through one or more intermediate components before becoming observable elsewhere.

A disruption affecting production can alter transport flows, inventories, distribution, market activity, and downstream operations.

Intermediate dependencies extend the reach of a shock across multiple system layers.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2586: Upstream Shock Propagation

Upstream shock propagation occurs when disturbance effects move toward suppliers, inputs, supporting functions, or earlier stages of an operational chain.

Reduced downstream activity, congestion, interrupted processing, or changing demand can alter conditions experienced by upstream components.

Shock effects can therefore move against the primary direction of physical or service flows.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2587: Downstream Shock Propagation

Downstream shock propagation occurs when disturbance effects move through successive dependent stages toward users, processes, networks, or destinations.

Loss of inputs, production, infrastructure, capacity, or connectivity can alter conditions across downstream activities.

Dependency chains create pathways through which downstream effects become distributed.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2588: Lateral Shock Propagation

Lateral shock propagation occurs when disturbance effects spread across parallel activities, locations, networks, or systems through shared resources or infrastructure.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2594: Temporal Shock Transmission

Temporal shock transmission describes how disturbance effects unfold across successive periods rather than appearing simultaneously throughout a system.

Inventories, buffers, transport times, processing cycles, contractual periods, reporting intervals, and operational schedules can delay observable effects.

Different system components can therefore encounter the same originating disturbance at different times.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2595: Shock Amplification

Shock amplification occurs when transmitted effects become greater as they move through connected system relationships.

High utilization, concentration, tight coupling, limited alternatives, accumulated constraints, feedback, or synchronized dependencies can increase the observed effect at subsequent stages.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2596: Shock Dampening

Shock dampening occurs when system structures reduce the intensity of a disturbance as its effects move through connected relationships.

Buffers, inventories, spare capacity, reserve capability, redundancy, alternative pathways, substitution, and operational flexibility can absorb or redistribute transmitted effects.

Dampening reduces propagation intensity without necessarily removing the originating disturbance.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2597: Shock Absorption

Shock absorption occurs when available system capability accommodates disturbance effects while maintaining defined functions.

Storage, reserves, redundant infrastructure, excess capacity, alternative resources, distributed networks, and adaptive operations can provide absorptive capability.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2598: Shock Redistribution

Shock redistribution occurs when disturbance effects shift across alternative routes, resources, nodes, locations, processes, or system layers.

Rerouting, substitution, capacity mobilization, inventory use, operational adjustment, or changing activity can alter where effects become concentrated.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2599: Propagation Boundaries

Propagation boundaries are points or conditions at which transmitted shock effects stop, weaken substantially, change form, or cease to remain operationally relevant.

Boundaries can arise from system separation, buffers, redundancy, alternative pathways, independent capacity, limited coupling, or disconnected dependencies.

Observed boundaries help distinguish the reach of a disturbance across interconnected structures.

### DGCP™ Macro Shot #2600: Shock Propagation Framework

Shock propagation connects origins, pathways, dependencies, coupling, networks, capacity, resources, flows, and time across interconnected systems.

Shocks can propagate directly, indirectly, upstream, downstream, laterally, and across system boundaries while network structures shape the distribution of transmitted effects.

Amplification, dampening, absorption, redistribution, and propagation boundaries describe how disturbance effects change as they move through connected structures.

Together these relationships reveal how rapid changes can extend beyond their point of origin while producing different effects across components, networks, locations, and time.

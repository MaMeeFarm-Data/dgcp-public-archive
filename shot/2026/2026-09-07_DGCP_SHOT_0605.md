Accumulation describes the build-up or reduction of a quantity, condition, resource, or state within a system over time.

Accumulation may involve materials, energy, information, inventory, capacity, backlog, debt, demand, reserves, or other measurable system conditions.

Identifying accumulation helps clarify how repeated inflows, outflows, gains, losses, or changes combine across time.

Observation identifies the quantity or condition being tracked.

Evidence supports what can be verified.

Context helps explain the conditions surrounding the observed change.

Inputs may add to an accumulated quantity.

Outputs, consumption, transfer, decay, or loss may reduce it.

When additions exceed reductions, accumulation may increase.

When reductions exceed additions, accumulation may decrease.

When additions and reductions are similar over a period, the accumulated quantity may remain relatively stable.

Accumulation may continue even when individual changes are small.

Delays may make the effect of accumulation visible only after repeated observations.

An observed increase or decrease does not by itself establish why the accumulation changed.

The same accumulated quantity may result from different combinations of inflows, outflows, timing, and system conditions.

Recording quantities, timing, inflows, outflows, relevant conditions, and available evidence supports traceability and helps distinguish observed accumulation from assumptions about mechanism.

---

## Principle

Identify what is accumulating.

Record the quantity or condition.

Observe what enters.

Observe what leaves.

Compare change over time.

Preserve timing and context.

Do not infer mechanism without evidence.

Maintain traceability.

Review accumulation as inflows, outflows, or system conditions change.

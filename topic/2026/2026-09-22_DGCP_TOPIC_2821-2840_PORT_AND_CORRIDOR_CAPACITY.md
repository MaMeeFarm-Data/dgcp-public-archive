### DGCP™ Topic #2821: Port & Corridor Capacity

Port and corridor capacity connects major transport gateways with the inland and maritime pathways used to move goods between production, trade, storage, and consumption systems.

Ports, terminals, roads, railways, waterways, pipelines, border crossings, and logistics facilities operate as connected physical structures.

Cargo movement therefore depends on capacity distributed across gateways and the corridors extending beyond them.

Port and corridor capacity remains structurally observable.

Seaport capacity connects maritime transport with the physical infrastructure used to receive, handle, store, and dispatch cargo.

Berths, channels, terminals, cranes, yards, warehouses, gates, road links, rail links, and marine services interact within port systems.

Maritime shipping capacity therefore remains distinct from the capacity of ports to process vessels and cargo.

### DGCP™ Topic #2823: Port Berth Capacity

Port berth capacity connects arriving vessels with physical locations where loading, unloading, transfer, and servicing can occur.

Berth length, water depth, terminal configuration, cargo type, handling equipment, and vessel characteristics interact with usable berth capacity.

Port throughput therefore depends partly on the number and characteristics of vessel interfaces available within terminal systems.

### DGCP™ Topic #2839: Multi-Layer Port & Corridor Capacity Constraints

Multi-layer port and corridor capacity constraints occur where cargo movement is bounded simultaneously by maritime access, berths, terminals, yards, gates, inland connections, transport routes, border interfaces, and logistics nodes.

Capacity available at one gateway or corridor segment does not independently determine the throughput of the complete transport pathway.

Usable port and corridor capacity therefore emerges from connected capacity across sequential maritime, terminal, inland, and cross-border layers.

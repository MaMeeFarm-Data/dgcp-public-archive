## Purpose

This public learning whiteboard explains the difference between correlation and causation.

The objective is to help learners understand that two variables changing together does not, by itself, demonstrate that one variable causes the other.

## Observe

We may observe two variables changing together.

This relationship provides a starting point for further investigation.

---

## Correlation

Correlation describes an association or pattern between variables.

A correlation indicates that variables vary together in some way, but does not by itself establish why the relationship exists.

---

## Causation

Causation means that a change in one factor contributes to producing a change in another.

Establishing causation requires evidence beyond correlation alone.

---

## Investigate

When a correlation is observed, further investigation may examine:

- Temporal order
- Alternative explanations
- Plausible mechanisms
- Confounding variables
- Evidence across different situations
- Experimental or quasi-experimental evidence where appropriate

The purpose is to determine whether the observed relationship can reasonably support a causal interpretation.

---

## Key Checkpoints

Useful questions include:

- Do the variables change together?
- Which event or change happens first?
- Is there a plausible mechanism?
- Could another factor explain the relationship?
- Does the relationship remain observable in different situations?
- What happens when an intervention changes one of the relevant factors?

These questions help distinguish observed association from stronger causal evidence.

---

## Common Problems

### Spurious Correlation

Two variables may appear related because of coincidence or another underlying factor.

### Reverse Causation

The apparent direction of cause and effect may be reversed.

### Confounding Variables

A third factor may influence both variables and create or alter the observed relationship.

### Cherry Picking

Selecting only evidence that supports an existing belief can create a misleading interpretation.

---

## Examples

Ice cream sales and drowning incidents may both increase during warmer periods.

Their correlation does not mean that ice cream consumption causes drowning. A third factor, such as hot weather, can influence both.

The number of firefighters responding to a fire may correlate with the amount of damage because larger fires tend to require more firefighters.

The presence of a correlation alone therefore does not establish the causal direction or mechanism.

---

## Key Message

Correlation can reveal patterns worth investigating.

Causation requires stronger evidence about why a relationship occurs.

Do not confuse the two.

Observe the pattern.

Investigate the relationship.

Evaluate the evidence.

Then determine whether the result supports correlation, causation, or remains inconclusive.

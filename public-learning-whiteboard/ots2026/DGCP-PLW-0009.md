## Purpose

This public learning whiteboard explains how sampling allows us to study part of a population while also introducing the possibility of misleading conclusions.

The objective is to help learners understand that the quality of a conclusion depends not only on the collected data, but also on how the sample was selected and how well it represents the population being studied.

## Population

A population is the complete group or set that we want to understand.

Depending on the question, a population may consist of people, organizations, objects, events, measurements, transactions, or other units of interest.

---

## Sampling

A sample is a subset selected from a population for observation or analysis.

Sampling allows researchers and analysts to study a manageable portion of a larger population.

---

## Why We Sample

Studying an entire population may require substantial time, money, effort, or resources.

Sampling can make investigation more practical.

However, using only part of a population introduces uncertainty and the possibility that the selected sample does not adequately represent the whole.

---

## How Sampling Can Mislead

A sample can produce misleading conclusions when its composition differs systematically from the population relevant to the question.

Problems may arise when:

- The sample is too small
- Selection is not appropriately randomized
- Important groups are underrepresented
- The sampling location introduces bias
- The sampling method favors particular observations
- Relevant parts of the population are excluded

A large amount of data does not automatically correct a poorly designed sampling process.

---

## Representativeness

A useful sample should represent the characteristics of the population that matter for the question being investigated.

Representativeness depends on the population, sampling frame, selection method, response process, and purpose of the analysis.

No sample should be assumed to represent a population without examining how it was obtained.

---

## Check Before You Trust

Useful questions include:

- What is the population?
- How was the sample selected?
- Is the sample size appropriate for the purpose?
- Does the sample adequately represent relevant groups?
- Were some groups systematically excluded?
- Is the selection process random where appropriate?
- Could selection or response bias affect the result?

Understanding the sampling method is essential for interpreting the resulting statistics.

---

## Common Sampling Pitfalls

### Convenience Sampling

Observations are selected because they are easy to access.

Convenience may make data collection easier while producing a sample that differs systematically from the target population.

### Small Sample Size

Very small samples may provide unstable estimates and may fail to capture important variation within the population.

### Non-Random Selection

Selection procedures may systematically favor some members of the population over others.

### Survivorship Bias

Analysis may focus only on observations that remained visible or survived a selection process while ignoring those that disappeared or failed.

### Confirmation Bias

Sampling or interpretation may become distorted when evidence supporting an existing belief is preferentially selected or emphasized.

---

## Example

Suppose a survey asks people at a beach about their preferred ice cream flavor.

The resulting responses describe the surveyed beach visitors.

They do not automatically establish the preferences of an entire city, country, or broader population.

A broader sampling design that includes relevant groups from different locations and backgrounds may provide evidence that better represents the intended population.

---

## Key Insight

Sampling is powerful, but every sample has limitations.

The method used to select observations affects what the resulting data can reasonably tell us.

When interpreting a sample, examine both the results and the process that produced them.

---

## Key Message

A sample is a window into a population, not the entire population itself.

Better sampling supports better analysis.

Better analysis supports better understanding.

Always examine the sample before generalizing its results to the wider population.

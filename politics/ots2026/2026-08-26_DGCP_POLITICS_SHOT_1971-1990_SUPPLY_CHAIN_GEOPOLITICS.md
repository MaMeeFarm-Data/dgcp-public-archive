### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1971 — Supply Chain Geopolitics

Supply chain geopolitics connects production networks with trade, resources, infrastructure, technology, logistics, and interstate relationships.

Modern supply chains distribute production activities across multiple companies, facilities, and jurisdictions.

Governments and businesses operate within networks containing both domestic and external capabilities.

Supply chain structures therefore interact with wider political and economic systems.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1972 — Global Production Networks

Global production networks connect suppliers, manufacturers, processors, logistics providers, distributors, and markets across jurisdictions.

Different production stages can occur in separate geographic locations.

Materials and components may cross borders multiple times before reaching final markets.

Supply chain geography describes the spatial distribution of production, processing, storage, transportation, and market access.

Industrial activities concentrate differently according to resources, infrastructure, labor, technology, capital, and market conditions.

Geographic distribution creates connections among production centers and transportation networks.

Supply chain geography therefore forms part of the structure of international economic relationships.

Upstream networks connect resource systems with manufacturing structures.

Intermediate goods networks connect production stages through components, materials, and partially processed products.

Individual inputs can move between several facilities before final assembly.

Manufacturers therefore depend on specialized suppliers across multiple locations.

Intermediate goods form structural links within global production systems.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1976 — Final Assembly Networks

Final assembly networks combine components and intermediate goods into finished products.

Assembly facilities depend on upstream suppliers, logistics systems, workforce, infrastructure, energy, and market access.

Production can serve both domestic and international markets.

Final assembly therefore connects complex upstream networks with downstream distribution systems.

Final assembly networks remain observable.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1977 — Strategic Supply Chains

Strategic supply chains support goods, technologies, materials, or services considered important to national economic, industrial, technological, or infrastructure systems.

Different governments identify strategic sectors through their respective policy frameworks.

These supply chains can extend across multiple jurisdictions and production stages.

National priorities therefore interact with internationally distributed production networks.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1978 — Supply Chain Concentration

Supply chain concentration occurs when significant production capacity or supply originates from a limited number of companies, facilities, regions, or jurisdictions.

Concentration can exist at raw material, processing, component, manufacturing, or logistics stages.

Downstream industries can therefore depend on relatively concentrated upstream capabilities.

Supply chain concentration remains identifiable across global production systems.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1979 — Single-Source Dependency

Single-source dependency occurs when a defined input, component, service, or capability relies substantially on one supplier or production source.

Dependency can arise through specialization, technology, cost structures, contracts, geography, or limited alternatives.

The dependent activity remains connected to the continuity of the supplying node.

Single-source relationships therefore create identifiable structural exposure.

Geographic supply concentration occurs when production or processing capacity is clustered within a limited geographic area.

Concentration can reflect resource location, industrial ecosystems, infrastructure, workforce, technology, or historical investment.

International customers can depend on outputs originating from these concentrated areas.

Supply chain chokepoints are nodes or corridors through which significant material, component, product, or logistics flows pass.

Chokepoints can exist within production, processing, transportation, ports, shipping routes, border systems, or specialized industrial facilities.

Alternative routes and substitution capacity vary across individual networks.

Supply-chain structure therefore influences the significance of individual nodes.

Supply chains depend on the movement of inputs between production stages and markets.

Different industries maintain different timing and transportation requirements.

Maritime supply chain dependencies arise from the role of ocean transportation in connecting production and consumption across regions.

Ports, shipping services, vessels, terminals, maritime routes, and inland connections form interconnected logistics systems.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1984 — Supply Chain Diversification

Supply chain diversification distributes sourcing, production, processing, logistics, or other capabilities across multiple suppliers or locations.

Diversification can occur at different stages of production networks.

Companies and governments participate through sourcing, investment, industrial, and procurement decisions.

Dependencies remain present while their concentration can change.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1985 — Supply Chain Regionalization

Supply chain regionalization organizes greater portions of production and sourcing within defined geographic regions.

Regional networks can connect neighboring markets through trade agreements, transportation systems, investment, and industrial relationships.

Production remains capable of retaining connections with suppliers outside the region.

Regionalization therefore represents one configuration within wider global production networks.

Supply chain regionalization remains observable.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #1986 — Supply Chain Localization

Supply chain localization increases the domestic location of selected production, sourcing, processing, or supporting capabilities.

Governments and companies can pursue localization through different industrial, investment, procurement, or commercial mechanisms.

Domestic production continues to depend on external inputs in many sectors.

Localization therefore changes the geography of selected supply-chain stages without necessarily eliminating international connectivity.

Supply chain localization remains observable.

One economy can simultaneously supply important inputs to others while depending on external components or markets itself.

Production networks therefore create overlapping relationships of supply and dependency.

National industrial capacity and international connectivity coexist within these systems.

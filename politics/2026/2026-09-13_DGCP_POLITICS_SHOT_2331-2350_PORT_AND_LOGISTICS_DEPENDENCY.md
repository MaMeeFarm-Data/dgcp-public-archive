### DGCP™ Politics Shot #2331: Port & Logistics Dependency

Port and logistics dependency exists when political, economic, industrial, energy, food, or trade systems rely on ports and connected logistics infrastructure for movement between maritime and terrestrial networks.

Ports operate as transfer nodes linking vessels with terminals, storage, customs, roads, railways, pipelines, warehouses, and distribution systems.

The existence of maritime access alone does not establish equivalent logistics capability.

Port and logistics dependency therefore requires observation of both the port and its connected network.

Port and logistics dependency remains observable.

Berths, channels, breakwaters, terminals, cranes, yards, warehouses, pipelines, roads, and utility systems form different infrastructure layers.

Different ports therefore provide different functional capabilities.

Port infrastructure dependency reflects reliance on specialized fixed assets at maritime gateways.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #2350: Port & Logistics Dependency Architecture

Port and logistics dependency architecture describes the interconnected structure through which political, economic, industrial, energy, food, and trade systems rely on ports, terminals, cargo handling, storage, customs, digital systems, electricity, fuel, roads, railways, pipelines, inland hubs, intermodal connections, services, and workforce.

Port systems function as nodes within larger maritime and terrestrial networks rather than isolated coastal facilities.

Dependency can be infrastructural, operational, geographic, institutional, digital, energy-based, concentrated, distributed, direct, or embedded within wider supply systems.

The existence of port and logistics dependency does not by itself establish vulnerability, political control, coercion, congestion, disruption, or a specific political outcome.

Port and logistics dependency architecture therefore requires continuous separation of observable structural reliance from interpretation.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #2651: Cross-System Dependency

Cross-system dependency exists when the operation of one political, economic, industrial, technological, infrastructure, or social system relies on capabilities provided by another system.

Modern systems rarely operate as completely independent structures.

Energy, water, finance, logistics, communications, technology, labor, resources, and institutions can simultaneously support multiple activities.

Cross-system dependency therefore requires observation across system boundaries rather than within individual sectors alone.

Cross-system dependency remains observable.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #2668: Shared Dependency Structure

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #2669: Dependency Chain Structure

Dependency chain structure exists when one system relies on another system that itself relies on additional upstream or supporting systems.

A digital service can rely on compute, which relies on semiconductors, electricity, cooling, networks, industrial equipment, materials, logistics, finance, and skilled personnel.

Dependencies can therefore extend across multiple layers without being visible from the final service or capability.

Dependency chain mapping identifies structural relationships without establishing the probability, severity, or direction of future disruption.

### DGCP™ Politics Shot #2670: Cross-System Dependency Architecture

Cross-system dependency architecture describes the interconnected structure through which political, economic, industrial, technological, infrastructure, security, and social systems rely on capabilities located across other systems.

Energy connects with water and industry. Water connects with food and energy. Minerals connect with technology. Semiconductors connect with compute. Compute connects with electricity and digital infrastructure. Finance connects with infrastructure. Trade connects with logistics. Labor connects with industry and migration. Information connects with finance. Platforms connect with logistics. Standards connect with technology. Security connects with industrial capacity. Infrastructure connects with geography.

Dependencies can form reciprocal relationships, shared foundations, layered structures, dependency chains, concentrated nodes, and cross-system interfaces.

The existence of cross-system dependency does not by itself establish vulnerability, systemic risk, political control, coercion, cascading failure, strategic weakness, future disruption, or a specific political outcome.

Cross-system dependency architecture therefore requires continuous separation of observable structural relationships from interpretation.

The overall cross-system dependency architecture remains observable.
